# CS250 Agile and Scrum

## How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I learned that interpreting user needs involves figuring out what a request should actually accomplish before deciding how to build it. During the SNHU Travel project, one of my user stories involved allowing customers to narrow down vacations by price. Simply saying customers should be able to find affordable trips wouldn't have explained what the program needed to do. I had to consider how the results should respond to a specific budget, which also gave me something measurable to test later. Working through that example helped me understand why user stories need more than a description of what someone wants. They should give developers enough direction to build something useful without leaving the tester to guess what the expected result should be.

## How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

One thing I took away from this course is that I shouldn't assume the original requirements will stay the same throughout development. I ran into this during the SNHU Travel slideshow assignment when the agency changed its focus to wellness vacations. My first concern wasn't necessarily how to rewrite everything, but whether destinations that included a spa actually met the new expectations. I thought it made more sense to confirm that detail and revise one slide before spending time changing the rest. That experience changed how I think about approaching development because getting clarification early can prevent unnecessary work later. I would like to carry that approach into future projects through sprint planning, reviewing completed work with stakeholders, and using retrospectives to address problems that keep coming up.

## What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Before this course, I probably would have associated being a good team member mostly with completing my assigned work and helping when needed. The SNHU Travel assignments showed me that finishing my portion doesn't necessarily mean I've made things easier for everyone else. When the travel requirements changed, the developer needed clarification from the Product Owner, while the tester needed to know what behavior to check against the revised expectations. I encountered something similar when writing an email about how the system should respond to incomplete customer requests. Rather than deciding what the error message should say myself, I needed an answer from the Product Owner. I sometimes prefer figuring things out independently, but these assignments made me recognize when that habit could create more problems than it solves. To me, being a good team member means knowing when to ask, following through on responsibilities, and making sure other people aren't left working from assumptions I never bothered to clarify.
